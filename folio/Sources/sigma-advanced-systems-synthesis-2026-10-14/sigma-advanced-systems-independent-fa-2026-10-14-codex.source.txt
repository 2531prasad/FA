> [!THESIS]
> Sigma has a plausible way to turn acquired aerospace qualifications into a lower-cost manufacturing network. Its attractiveness as a security remains unproven: the reference valuation requires substantial profit growth, while cash conversion, minority attribution and completion of manufacturing approvals still need evidence. Research priority is justified; supported investment asymmetry is not established.

## Decision brief

**Working judgment:** Keep Sigma under investigation. Customer access and the emerging dual manufacturing footprint are potentially valuable, but contract visibility alone does not establish retained cash or justify the price. Later operating milestones strengthen the business case without resolving shareholder economics.

**Variant view:** The important asset may be the combination of existing OEM approvals and approved manufacturing relocation, rather than exposure to defence spending generally. Whether this is underappreciated is not established; the valuation already anticipates considerable success.

**Price hurdle:** At the ₹961.70 reference price and 18.9928952 crore disclosed shares, equity value is approximately ₹18,265 crore. Doubling with no dividends or dilution requires approximately ₹1,044 crore of annual parent-attributable earnings at a 35× terminal P/E. These are calculations, not targets or forecasts; the dated inputs and sensitivity appear below.

**Failure condition:** The thesis fails if the acquired qualifications support revenue but customers capture most relocation savings, UK costs remain stranded, or working capital and further equity issuance prevent durable growth in cash per share. This can happen while aerospace and defence demand grows.

**Next check:** Examine the next half-year accounts for an entity-level profit bridge, post-fundraise liquidity, cash collections and evidence of customer-accepted Sri City deliveries. Obtain a reconciliation of the EPS and cash-flow discrepancies identified below.

## The economic opportunity is approved work transfer

Sigma sells aerospace components and assemblies, defence electronics and subsystems, and related manufacturing capability. Its customers pay for qualified, reliable supply into demanding programmes. Nasmyth provides an acquired UK aerospace platform; Bromford expands engine-component capability; AS Strategic adds supply-chain access. The attachment describes these businesses and reports a Q1 FY27 revenue mix of approximately ₹229 crore aerospace, ₹104 crore export defence and ₹41 crore domestic defence. [Company presentation, August 2026](https://sigmaadvsys.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Investor-Presentation-Aug-2026-1.pdf).

The proposed mechanism is:

**Long-lived aircraft and defence programmes → qualified supplier demand → acquired customer approvals → approved work transfer to India → lower delivered cost → retained margin → cash per share.**

The final three links matter most. A certified facility is not automatically approved for every part, process or programme. Lower Indian labour cost is not the same as lower total delivered cost after tooling, scrap, supervision, logistics, duplicate capacity and customer price concessions.

| Potential bottleneck | How Sigma could capture value | How capture could erode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qualified production history | Remain embedded in existing programmes | OEM qualifies another source or reprices contracts |
| Complex machining and special processes | Expand content supplied per programme | Capex, yield losses and subcontracting absorb margins |
| Approved India–UK manufacturing route | Retain part of the cost reduction | Customer takes savings; UK capacity remains underused |
| Defence programme access | Convert relationships into repeat orders | Procurement timing, export approvals and replenishment cycles |

These are underwriting mechanisms, not verified monopoly claims. The presentation's assertions of four-year-plus switching cycles and single-source or dominant positions require programme-level corroboration. High switching costs can protect incumbent revenue while a concentrated OEM customer still limits price increases.

### Repeat technical demand does not guarantee recurring supplier profit

New aircraft production and maintenance generate different purchasing patterns. Replacement requirements depend on utilisation, approved repair limits, component condition and engine shop visits; an entire supplier catalogue is not replaced at each service event. Sigma's split between original equipment, spare parts and aftermarket work was not established.

Similarly, ammunition replenishment can create repeat physical demand without repeat orders on the same margins. Counter-drone systems may require software and hardware upgrades, but this report assigns no recurring subscription value without contract terms, ownership rights and evidence that Sigma receives the associated economics.

## Macro demand is supportive; the serviceable market is narrower

Customer disclosures support an aerospace demand foundation. Airbus delivered 351 commercial aircraft in H1 2026 versus 306 a year earlier, a calculated increase of 14.7%, and reported a 9,222-aircraft backlog. Its planned production ramp is forward-looking, and the backlog spans programmes beyond Sigma's disclosed exposure. These figures establish demand and execution pressure, not Sigma's market share. [Airbus H1 2026 results, 29 July 2026](https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-07-airbus-reports-half-year-h1-2026-results).

Rolls-Royce reported H1 large-engine flying hours up 4% and large-engine MRO output up 13%. It also described continuing supply-chain costs. More utilisation can support replacement-component demand, but OEM service revenue also contains pricing and contract adjustments; its growth cannot be translated directly into Sigma component volumes. [Rolls-Royce H1 2026 results, 30 July 2026](https://www.rolls-royce.com/investors/results-reports-and-presentations/financial-results/rr-holdings-plc-2026-half-year-results.aspx).

Defence spending provides a separate conditional tailwind. India's FY27 budget earmarks ₹1.85 lakh crore for capital acquisition, including approximately ₹1.39 lakh crore for domestic procurement. These are budget allocations, not supplier orders, and much of the spending lies outside Sigma's products. [Government of India budget backgrounder, 3 February 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2222601&lang=1&reg=3). NATO's 2035 commitment includes at least 3.5% of GDP for core defence and up to 1.5% for wider security-related spending; it is a future political commitment, not a current equipment market of that size. [NATO explanation](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/introduction-to-nato/defence-expenditures-and-natos-5-commitment?selectedLocale=fr).

### Bound the opportunity by qualified work, not total aircraft or defence spending

For this study, the economically relevant TAM is annual customer spending on Sigma-addressable components, subsystems and manufacturing processes in qualified programmes. A defensible bottom-up estimate would multiply programme production and replacement volumes by eligible content per unit, then constrain it by supplier share and approved capacity. Those content, volume and share data are unavailable; a numerical global TAM is therefore not established.

The attachment's ₹8,000 crore-plus order book is company-reported multiyear business visibility, not annual market size or guaranteed revenue. As an illustration only, spreading ₹8,000 crore over seven years gives approximately ₹1,143 crore annually. Contract durations, call-offs and product mix differ, so this is not a revenue forecast and should not be added to current sales.

The 25 August release announced a further £125 million Rolls-Royce agreement, approximately ₹1,600 crore, alongside the earlier approximately £300 million agreement. It describes India and UK sourcing as directed by Rolls-Royce. The relationship is encouraging, but the disclosed amounts cannot simply be added to the old order book without checking inclusion, renewal and recognition schedules. Minimum purchase obligations and cancellation protection were not verified. [Additional Rolls-Royce agreement](https://nsearchives.nseindia.com/corporate/MEGASOFT_25082026073445_Disclosure_Reg_30_-_20260825-signed.pdf).

These scenarios test economic requirements; none is a forecast or assigned a probability. Terminal operating figures represent FY32 annual earnings estimates used at the October 2031 valuation date. Revenue is consolidated; EBITDA excludes disposal gains and other non-operating income. Each case assumes no additional acquisitions requiring separate funding.

| Operating assumption, INR crore unless % | Stalled transfer | Strong execution | Hurdle-scale execution |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 2,000 | 4,000 | 6,500 |
| EBITDA margin | 16% | 23% | 26% |
| Depreciation / interest | 60 / 45 | 100 / 50 | 150 / 60 |
| Tax rate / minority PAT | 25% / 10 | 25% / 30 | 25% / 100 |

The stalled case allows demand to grow while duplicated costs prevent margin improvement. Strong execution assumes substantial order renewal, India ramp-up, good yields and retained savings. The last case requires a much larger qualified business and a margin above the starting blended level; procurement announcements and initial capacity alone do not support it. Tax, depreciation, interest and minority amounts are explicit analytical assumptions, not company guidance.

```text
Group PAT proxy  = (revenue × EBITDA margin − depreciation − interest)
                   × (1 − tax rate)
Parent PAT proxy = group PAT proxy − minority PAT
Terminal price  = parent PAT proxy / terminal crore shares × terminal P/E
```

The middle case demonstrates the central risk: a much larger and more profitable business can leave shareholder returns modest if today's valuation compresses. The hurdle case produces upside mathematically, but lacks sufficient operating support to establish asymmetry. Its generous terminal multiple is another dependency: at 30×, the same earnings produce approximately ₹1,595, or 1.66×.

### Earnings must survive the reinvestment bill

As a cash sanity check, the middle case generates ₹577.5 crore group PAT before minorities. Adding ₹100 crore depreciation and subtracting assumed ₹180 crore capex, ₹100 crore incremental working capital and ₹15 crore lease principal leaves ₹382.5 crore group cash before minority distributions. An illustrative ₹30 crore minority distribution leaves ₹352.5 crore, subject to cash location and remittance constraints. This simplified proxy omits other noncash adjustments and is not a forecast of distributable cash.

Those reinvestment assumptions require verification. Capex below the amount needed for qualified expansion would overstate cash; receivable delays could consume considerably more working capital. A 10% larger terminal share count reduces all per-share values by 9.1% at unchanged profit, while a 20% shortfall in parent earnings reduces the hurdle case to approximately 1.77× at the same multiple. No dividends are assumed because surplus cash available for distribution is unproven.

## Try to break the investment while the macro thesis succeeds

**Strong demand, weak retention:** Aircraft output and defence purchases grow, but OEMs exchange additional volume for lower unit prices. Transferred work leaves UK overhead behind, while new India processes need more capital and supervision than expected. Sales rise without commensurate cash per share.

**Profitable orders, financing strain:** Large orders require materials, inventory and receivables before collection. Acceptance delays or a reduced receivables facility force costly borrowing or more shares. The security suffers even if every product ultimately sells.

**Technical relevance, limited ownership economics:** An associate, consortium or partially owned subsidiary wins a high-profile programme. Sigma receives only its contractual manufacturing margin or ownership share. Valuing the entire programme as parent earnings overstates the payoff.

**Successful business, excessive entry expectations:** Relocation works and earnings grow, but growth matures and the multiple falls. The middle scenario quantifies this failure path without requiring a macro reversal.

The macro premise can fail separately. Airline economics, financing constraints, geopolitical disruption or OEM production problems could slow aircraft purchases. Defence budgets may be delayed or redirected; replenishment may normalise; export authorisations may restrict execution. Monitor actual deliveries and funded orders rather than treating budget targets as realised demand.

## Open questions and verification scope

The following checks have the greatest decision value:

1. **At the next half-year release:** reconcile group and parent earnings, weighted-average shares, AS Strategic ownership effects, associates and minorities. Resolve the printed EPS discrepancy with the company or auditor.
2. **Before relying on liquidity:** reconcile the two FY26 cash-flow versions; obtain post-raise cash, debt, restricted balances, covenants, receivables-facility recourse and acquisition payment obligations. Reconcile lease disclosures and avoid double-counting guarantees.
3. **For the Sri City thesis:** establish formal customer acceptance, part-specific source approvals, special-process accreditation, accepted serial output, utilisation, scrap and the net savings retained after customer concessions and UK backfill costs.
4. **For the next twelve months of defence execution:** verify deliveries, cash receipts, customer acceptance and the exact economic share of consortium and associate orders. Seek repeat orders rather than extrapolating one replenishment contract indefinitely.
5. **For a supported 2× case:** reconcile contracted demand, qualified capacity and incremental capital with the required attributable earnings. If evidence cannot bridge that gap, retain the conclusion that the security's asymmetry is unproven.
